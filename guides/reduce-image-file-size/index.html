How to Reduce Image File Size Without Ruining Quality

Upload forms often reject images over a limit such as 500 KB or 2 MB. The quickest fix is rarely to drag a quality slider to the left. File size depends on three things, in this order of importance: how many pixels the image has, which format it is saved in, and how hard the encoder compresses it. Work through them in that order and you will lose the least quality.

Step 1: Start from the right number of pixels

The size of an image file grows with its pixel count. A 12-megapixel camera photo of 4000 × 3000 pixels contains 12 million pixels. If you will show it on a page 1200 pixels wide, the same photo at 1200 × 900 contains 1.08 million pixels, only 9% as many. Most of the bytes in the original are wasted on detail that the screen can never display.

Uncompressed, each pixel takes three bytes (red, green and blue). The 12-megapixel photo is therefore about 36 MB before any compression, and the 1200 × 900 version is about 3.2 MB. Compression then shrinks both, but starting from fewer pixels always wins.

Decide the largest size you actually need, then use Resize Image with the aspect ratio lock on. Common targets are 1920 pixels wide for full-screen web use, 1200 for a blog image, and 800 for an email or thumbnail.

Step 2: Crop what you do not need

Cropping removes pixels that carry no value. A portrait with a large empty background can often be cropped by a third without losing anything important, and every removed pixel is a byte saved. Crop first and resize second, so that you do not resize pixels you are about to throw away.

Step 3: Choose the format

For photographs, JPG is normally the smallest option that still looks good. For graphics with flat colors or text, PNG can actually be smaller than JPG and looks sharper. A screenshot saved as JPG can be larger and worse than the same screenshot as PNG. The guide on JPG, PNG and WebP explains the rules in detail.

Step 4: Set the compression quality

With the dimensions and format right, use Image Compressor. The quality slider runs from 10% to 100%. For photographs, values between 70% and 85% usually look nearly identical to the original at normal viewing size while saving a large share of the file size. Below about 50% you begin to see blocks and banding, especially in skies and skin.

If you have a hard limit, type it into the optional maximum size field. The tool tries up to nine qualities between 10% and your slider value, using a binary search, and keeps the best one that fits. If even 10% cannot reach the limit, the tool tells you to reduce the dimensions first, which brings you back to Step 1.

A worked example

Suppose a form accepts files up to 500 KB, and your photo is 4032 × 3024 pixels at 3.8 MB. Resizing to 1600 pixels wide (1600 × 1200) cuts the pixel count to about 16% of the original. Compressing the result at 80% quality often lands close to or under 500 KB for an ordinary photograph, though busy scenes come out larger. If it does not fit, use the size target field rather than lowering the slider by guesswork. Check the result at 100% zoom before you submit it.

File sizes depend on the content. A photo of a clear sky compresses far more than a photo of a crowd or a tree in leaf, so the numbers above are illustrations, not guarantees.

Mistakes that make quality worse

  • Compressing a file that has already been compressed. Each JPG save loses a little more detail. Always go back to the original.
  • Using a transparent PNG as a JPG without choosing a background. The transparent area is filled with white.
  • Lowering quality when the real problem is the pixel count. A huge image at 40% quality looks worse than a smaller image at 80%, and may still be larger.
  • Judging only at the zoomed-out view. Flaws show at 100%. Look at both.
