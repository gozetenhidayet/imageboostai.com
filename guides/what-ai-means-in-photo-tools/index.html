What "AI" Means in Photo Tools: Models, Filters and How to Tell the Difference

The word AI is attached to almost every photo tool today, sometimes accurately and often not. Knowing the difference between a trained model and an ordinary filter helps you predict what a tool can do, how it can fail, and whether your photo leaves your device. This page explains the categories and states plainly how each ImageBoostAI tool works.

Three kinds of technology that all get called AI

Classical image processing. Mathematical operations on pixels: blurring, sharpening, changing brightness, stretching levels, blending colors, resizing. The same input always gives the same output, and the result contains only information that was in your photo. Most photo tools, including many called AI, are in this category.

Recognition models. A model trained on labeled examples that classifies what it sees: which pixels are a person, which letters are in a line of text. It does not create new content. It makes a decision about existing content. Background removal and OCR belong here.

Generative models. A model that creates new pixels that were never in your photo, such as extending a scene, replacing an object with a different one or drawing a background from a text prompt. These are the tools that honestly deserve the label generative AI. They normally need a lot of computing power, so they usually run on a server, which means your image is uploaded to someone else's computer.

Why the difference matters

  • Predictability. A filter does exactly what its math says. A generative model can invent details, including wrong ones, such as a distorted hand or a changed face.
  • Privacy. A tool that runs in your browser can work without uploading your picture. A server-based generative tool must receive it.
  • Truthfulness. A repaired or extended image may contain content that never existed. That is acceptable for art and unacceptable for evidence, documents or news.
  • Expectations. If a tool called AI Inpainting only averages nearby colors, it will fail on a large object, and you should know that before you spend time on it.

How to spot an exaggerated claim

  • The tool is described as AI but gives the same result for the same input every time and has no prompt. That suggests a filter.
  • A large missing area is filled with plausible content in seconds, with no upload and no waiting. A real generative model usually cannot do that in a few seconds inside a typical phone or laptop browser.
  • The page never says what model it uses, where it runs, or what it cannot do.
  • There are claims such as one-click restoration or perfect results with no limits listed.

How each ImageBoostAI tool works

ImageBoostAI uses no generative model. Some of the tool names were chosen years ago and still use the word AI because people search for it. The pages describe the real method for that reason. Here is the breakdown.

In short: six tools use a recognition model, and none of them generates new image content. If a tool's name suggests more than that, its page and its panel say so.

What runs where

All processing happens in your browser. Two kinds of files are downloaded from public content networks the first time you need them: the segmentation model and its TensorFlow.js runtime for the background tools, and the Tesseract engine with a language file for OCR. HEIC photos need a decoder, which is also downloaded on demand. Your own image is not sent to a server for editing. The privacy policy lists the third-party services that can be contacted.

A fair way to judge any tool

Ask three questions. Does it change only what I asked it to change? Does it add anything that was not in my photo? Can I tell from the page how it works and what it cannot do? Tools that answer these clearly are worth using, whatever name they carry.
