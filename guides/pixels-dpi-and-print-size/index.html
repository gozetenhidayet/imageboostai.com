Pixels, DPI and Print Size: What Resolution Do You Actually Need?

Resolution is one of the most confused topics in image editing. People ask for a 300 DPI image as if DPI were a property of the file that can be switched on. In practice there are only two numbers that matter: the pixel dimensions of the image, and the physical size you want to print it at. DPI is just the ratio between them.

Pixels are the only real size

A digital photo is a grid of pixels. A picture that is 3000 pixels wide and 2000 pixels tall contains 6 million of them. That grid is the information. Everything else, including the number labeled DPI or PPI in the file's metadata, describes how the grid should be spread over a physical area when it is printed.

On a screen, the metadata does not matter at all. A web page or a phone shows each image pixel using a number of screen pixels that the layout decides. Whether the file says 72 DPI or 300 DPI makes no difference on screen.

The formula

Printed size in inches equals pixels divided by DPI. Rearranged, the pixels you need equal the print size in inches multiplied by the DPI you want.

A 12-megapixel camera photo of 4000 × 3000 pixels printed at 300 DPI produces an image 13.3 × 10 inches. The same file at 150 DPI covers 26.7 × 20 inches, with each pixel spread twice as wide.

How much DPI is enough?

300 DPI is the common target for photographs viewed at arm's length, such as photo prints and magazines. Large prints that are viewed from farther away, such as posters and banners, look fine at much lower values, because the eye cannot resolve individual pixels at a distance. A billboard may be a tiny fraction of 300 DPI.

A practical approach is to ask how far the viewer will be, and check whether the pixels you have reach roughly 150 to 300 DPI at the planned print size. Below about 100 DPI at close range, pixels start to be visible.

Millimeters and passport-style sizes

Many official sizes are given in millimeters. Convert to inches by dividing by 25.4, then multiply by DPI. A 35 × 45 mm photo is 1.378 × 1.772 inches, which at 300 DPI is about 413 × 531 pixels. A 2 × 2 inch photo is 600 × 600 pixels. These are exactly the values used by the country presets in Resize Image, which crop from the center to the right shape and then scale to the pixel size.

A correct pixel size is only one requirement. Authorities also specify background, head size, expression and print or file rules, and these change. Always check the issuing authority's current instructions.

What to do when you do not have enough pixels

If an image has too few pixels for the size you want, there are only two honest options: use it smaller, or enlarge it and accept that it will look soft. Enlarging, also called upscaling, creates new pixels by calculation. It cannot add real detail. The guide on why upscaling cannot add detail explains this, and Image Upscaler gives you 2×, 4× and 8× with a 6,000-pixel limit on the longest side.

The better solution is to obtain a larger original: re-shoot with a higher resolution setting, re-scan at 600 DPI, or request the source file.

Checklist before you print

  1. Find the pixel dimensions of your image. The status line in the editor shows them after upload.
  2. Divide the width and the height by the DPI you want, to get the largest size you can print well.
  3. If that size is smaller than you need, decide whether the viewing distance allows a lower DPI.
  4. Crop to the print shape first, then resize to the exact pixel size.
  5. Keep your original file and export a copy for printing.
