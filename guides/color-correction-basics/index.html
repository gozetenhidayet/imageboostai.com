Color Correction Basics: Exposure, Contrast, White Balance and Saturation

Most photos improve with a small amount of correction, and most bad edits come from changing too many things at once. Learning what each control does, and doing them in a sensible order, lets you fix a photo in a couple of minutes and avoid the overprocessed look.

The controls, in plain terms

  • Exposure is the overall amount of light. Raising it brightens everything. In photography it is measured in stops, and one stop is a doubling of light. In Color Correction, +100 on the Exposure slider is one stop brighter and −100 is one stop darker.
  • Contrast is the difference between the dark and light parts. High contrast looks punchy and can lose detail at the extremes. Low contrast looks flat or hazy.
  • Shadows and highlights let you brighten the dark areas or darken the bright areas without moving the middle tones as much. They are useful for pictures with a bright sky and a dark foreground.
  • Temperature moves the whole picture between cool (blue) and warm (orange). It is the main control for white balance.
  • Tint moves between green and magenta. It corrects the casts that temperature cannot, such as the green of fluorescent light.
  • Saturation is the intensity of color. At 0% the image is gray, and above 100% the colors become more vivid and less natural.

Exposure, brightness and shadows: what is the difference?

Exposure in Color Correction and Brightness in Brightness & Contrast both multiply every pixel value by the same factor. Exposure is expressed in stops, where +100 doubles the light. Brightness is expressed as a percentage, where 100% is neutral and 200% doubles it. Because the change is proportional, bright areas get brighter in absolute terms as well, so raising either one on a photo with a dark foreground and a bright sky can push the sky to pure white before the foreground looks right.

That is the situation where Shadows is the better control. It lifts only the darker tones, with the effect fading out toward the middle, and leaves bright areas nearly alone. A moderate exposure increase combined with a little more Shadows usually looks better than a large global increase. Some other editors implement Brightness as adding a constant, which turns blacks gray; these two tools do not behave that way. Brightness & Contrast is handy for quick changes and for converting to grayscale by setting Saturation to 0%.

A workflow that works

  1. Crop first so that the edges you will discard do not influence your judgment.
  2. Fix exposure. Aim for a picture that shows detail in the important areas, not for a perfect histogram.
  3. Recover shadows and highlights. Lift Shadows a little to reveal detail, and lower Highlights if the sky is blown out. Watch for gray, flat blacks.
  4. Fix the color cast. Find something that should be neutral, such as a white wall, gray pavement or a white shirt, and adjust Temperature and Tint until it looks neutral.
  5. Set contrast and saturation last, in small amounts. Compare with the original.
  6. Sharpen last, at the final size, if needed.

The Auto-Enhance button and Photo Enhancer

Color Correction has an Auto-Enhance button. It does not analyze your photo. It sets fixed, gentle values (exposure +10, shadows +15, highlights −10, temperature +5) and applies them, which makes it a decent starting point for an average photo, and a poor choice for one with a strong problem.

Photo Enhancer works differently. It looks at your photo's actual brightness range in each color channel, ignoring the most extreme 1% at each end, stretches that range, and blends 65% of the result with the original. It is a one-click fix for a flat, hazy photo, and it can correct a mild color cast. It is less suitable for photos that are mostly one color, such as a sunset, because the statistics reflect that dominant color.

Common mistakes

  • Correcting on a screen with night mode or a strong color filter. Turn filters off while you edit.
  • Over-saturating. If skin looks orange or foliage glows, reduce it.
  • Trying to recover clipped areas. Where the sky is pure white or a shadow is pure black, no detail was recorded, and no slider will bring it back.
  • Correcting each photo of a set differently. Use similar settings so the group looks consistent.

A note on screens and print

These tools adjust how an image looks on a screen. They do not read or apply ICC color profiles, so they are not a replacement for a color-managed workflow. Colors can look different on another display or in print. For critical print work, use calibrated equipment and software that manages color profiles.
