Natural Portrait Retouching: Soften Skin Without the Plastic Look

Retouching is easy to overdo. The most common complaint about edited portraits is not that the person looks bad, but that they look like a different, slightly artificial version of themselves. A few principles keep a portrait polished and believable.

The goal is to remove distractions, not character

Decide what you are fixing before you open a tool. Typical goals are a temporary blemish, shine from flash, uneven skin tone, or dull eyes. Permanent features such as freckles, laugh lines and moles are part of how a person looks, and removing them is rarely what the subject wants.

Skin has texture at several scales, and viewers read that texture as realism. A blur that removes it all makes a face look like a mask, even if the face is otherwise perfect. Keep some texture visible at 100% zoom.

Treat selected areas, not the whole picture

Whole-image filters change everything that matches their rule, including things that are not skin. Skin Smoothing's Quick Smooth and Portrait Enhancer, for example, identify skin by color alone. They have no face detection, so a warm wooden table or a sandy beach can be softened along with the face. They are fine for a quick first pass on a portrait with a neutral background and are not the right tool when the background has warm tones.

Brush-based tools put you in control. Face Retouch blends in a softened, slightly brighter copy only where you paint, at 75% strength. Paint cheeks, forehead and chin, and keep the brush off eyelashes, eyebrows, lips and the hairline, where blur reads as a smear.

Handling blemishes

For a single spot, use the Heal Spot option in Skin Smoothing. Drag a small box slightly larger than the spot and press Heal Spot. The tool replaces that small ellipse with a blurred patch from the surroundings. Use small selections, one spot at a time, and check each one. A large box leaves a visible blurred disk, and spots close to edges, such as an eyebrow, are better left alone.

Eyes and teeth: less is more

Eyes are the first place viewers look, and small changes are noticeable. Eye Enhancer increases contrast, saturation and brightness inside the area you paint. Paint only the iris and the visible detail, treat both eyes in the same way, and keep the pupil and the small reflection of light, the catch-light, visible. If either disappears, the eyes look flat and lifeless.

For teeth, the goal is to remove a yellow cast, not to make them white. Teeth that are brighter than the whites of the eyes look artificial. Teeth Whitening brightens and reduces saturation inside the painted area, so paint only the teeth and keep it off the gums and lips. Compare with the eye whites and the collar of a white shirt as a reference.

A workflow that stays natural

  1. Fix exposure and color first with Color Correction, because skin issues often come from lighting or color cast.
  2. Remove obvious distractions: individual blemishes with Heal Spot, stray marks with Object Remover.
  3. Soften only where needed with Face Retouch, with small brush strokes and one pass.
  4. Make small adjustments to eyes and teeth, if at all.
  5. View the result at the size it will be shown and at 100%. Compare with the original, then undo anything that looks processed.

When not to retouch

Identity documents, visa photos and similar official pictures have rules against altering a person's appearance. Retouching can lead to rejection and can be treated as misrepresentation. Check the requirements of the authority before you edit.

More broadly, think about the context. A light cleanup for a professional profile is common and widely accepted. Heavy changes to someone's face or body without their agreement are not, and are worth discussing with the person first.
