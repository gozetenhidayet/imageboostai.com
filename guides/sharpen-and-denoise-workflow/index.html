Sharpening and Noise Reduction: A Practical Workflow

Sharpening and noise reduction pull in opposite directions. Sharpening raises contrast at edges, which also raises the visibility of grain. Noise reduction smooths grain, which also smooths fine detail. Getting a good result is mostly a matter of doing the steps in the right order and stopping early.

What noise and softness really are

Noise is random variation in pixel values. It comes from the camera sensor, especially in low light, and from scanning film or paper. It appears as speckle in flat areas, such as skies and walls.

Softness is the opposite problem: edges are not abrupt enough. It has several causes, including slight misfocus, a small amount of camera shake, lens limits, and the smoothing that happens whenever an image is resized or compressed. Mild softness can be improved. Heavy motion blur or a missed focus point cannot be reversed, because the information is not in the file.

How an unsharp mask works

The name sounds odd, but the idea is simple. The tool makes a blurred copy of the image, subtracts it from the original to find where edges are, and adds that difference back to the original, scaled by a strength value. At an edge the difference is large, so the edge gets more contrast. In a flat area the difference is near zero, so little changes.

In Image Sharpener, the blurred copy uses a 2-pixel blur and the Sharpness slider scales the difference, from none at 0% to 1.6 times at 100%. Remove Blur uses the same method with fixed settings: a 2.2-pixel blur and a gain of 1.35.

When the strength is too high, the added contrast overshoots on either side of an edge and shows up as a bright line next to a dark line. This artifact is called a halo, and it is the clearest sign of over-sharpening.

How noise reduction works here

Noise Remover blends a blurred copy of the image into the original. The Strength slider sets both the blur radius, from 1 to 4.5 pixels, and the share of blurred image mixed in, up to 75%. Because the filter does not follow edges, it softens detail along with noise. That is why moderate settings are better, and why the technique works best on grainy but simple images.

The order that works

  1. Crop first. There is no reason to process pixels you will discard.
  2. Correct exposure and color. Brightening a dark photo increases visible noise, so adjust tone before judging the noise level.
  3. Reduce noise lightly. Zoom to 100% on a flat area such as sky and apply a low strength, around 25 to 40%.
  4. Resize to the final dimensions. Resizing down averages pixels, which already hides some noise and some detail.
  5. Sharpen last, at the final size, starting with a low strength and checking edges at 100%.

The reason for sharpening last is that resizing softens edges. If you sharpen first and then shrink, much of the work is lost and any halos are shrunk too. If you sharpen first and then enlarge, halos are enlarged.

Judging the result

  • Look at 100%, but also at the size people will see. Grain that is obvious at 100% may vanish at a smaller size, and halos that are invisible in a small preview show up on a large screen.
  • Check high-contrast edges, such as a dark branch against a bright sky, for light outlines.
  • Check textures. Hair, fabric and foliage that turn smooth and waxy mean the noise reduction was too strong.
  • Compare with the original often. After a few minutes your eye adapts and everything looks fine.

When these tools are not the answer

If the photo is badly out of focus or smeared by motion, no sharpening setting will rescue it, and pushing it creates noise and halos. Re-shoot if you can. If the problem is low resolution rather than softness, see the guide on why upscaling cannot add detail. For scans of faded prints, AI Photo Restore combines light noise blending, a contrast stretch and mild sharpening in one step, and it is worth trying before manual tuning.

These are classical image-processing filters, not trained models. They are predictable, they run on your device, and they do exactly what is described above, with no guessing about what the picture should have contained.
