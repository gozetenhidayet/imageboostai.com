Cropping and Aspect Ratios: Frame Your Photos for Any Use

A crop decides what the viewer sees, and the aspect ratio decides what shape the picture can take. Most cropping problems come from choosing the shape too late, after the photo has been composed for something else. Understanding ratios makes it easy to frame a photo for a post, a print or a screen.

What an aspect ratio is

An aspect ratio is the proportion of width to height, written as two numbers. A square is 1:1. A ratio does not say how large the picture is, only its shape. A 1000 × 1000 image and a 4000 × 4000 image are both 1:1.

To find the ratio of an image, divide its width by its height. 4000 × 3000 is 4:3 because 4000 ÷ 3000 = 1.333, the same as 4 ÷ 3. 1920 × 1080 is 16:9 because both give 1.778.

Common ratios and where they are used

RatioTypical use
1:1Profile pictures and square grid posts
4:3Many compact cameras and phone default photos, presentation slides
3:2Most camera sensors, standard 4 × 6 inch prints
16:9Widescreen video, monitors, video thumbnails
9:16Vertical video, stories and short clips
2:3Portrait-oriented prints and posters

Platforms change their recommended sizes from time to time, so check the current guideline for the one you are using, but these ratios cover most needs.

The arithmetic of cropping

Cropping to a ratio keeps the full length of one side and trims the other. From a 4000 × 3000 photo, a 1:1 crop is 3000 × 3000, because the height limits it. A 9:16 vertical crop from the same photo has height 3000 and width 3000 × 9 ÷ 16, which is about 1688 pixels, so you keep less than half the width. A 16:9 crop has width 4000 and height 4000 × 9 ÷ 16 = 2250 pixels.

This is why a horizontal photo is a poor starting point for a tall vertical image. You will discard most of it. When you can, shoot in the orientation you will need.

Composition tips

  • Rule of thirds. Imagine the picture divided into a three-by-three grid. Placing the subject near a line, or at an intersection, usually looks more dynamic than dead center.
  • Headroom. Leave a modest gap above a person's head. Cutting through the top of the head looks cramped, while too much space makes the subject seem small.
  • Do not cut at joints. Cropping through wrists, knees or ankles looks awkward. Crop between joints, or include the whole limb.
  • Leave a margin. Platforms often add rounded corners, overlays or a second crop. Keep important content away from the edges.
  • Straighten the horizon before cropping. Rotation is in the same panel, and cropping after straightening gives a clean edge.

Crop first, resize second

Every pixel you crop away is data you will not use, so crop before you resize or edit. Then use Resize Image to set the exact pixel size. If you crop to the ratio you need and then resize with the aspect lock on, the result has the right shape and size without any stretching. Stretching, which means setting a width and height that do not match the ratio, distorts faces and circles.

When you need more room than the photo has

If you need a wider or taller picture than your crop allows, for example to leave room for a headline, AI Outpainting can extend the canvas by 5 to 50% on each side. The new border is a mirrored and blurred continuation of the edges, not new scenery, so it works on skies, plain walls and blurred backgrounds, and shows repeated shapes on busy edges. A small extension is usually enough. It increases the pixel dimensions: 20% on a 1000 × 800 image gives 1400 × 1120.

For anything more elaborate, shoot wider next time, or keep the original and design the layout around the crop you have.
